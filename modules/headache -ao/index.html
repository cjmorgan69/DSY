Clinical Context

Headache is one of the most common presentations in pre-hospital care. Most are benign — tension-type or migraine. The critical skill for AOs is identifying the small number of presentations that are time-critical and potentially life-threatening.

These "red flag" headaches include subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH), meningitis, stroke, and raised intracranial pressure. Missing a red flag headache can be fatal. The Headache — AO protocol gives you a structured approach to assessment and escalation.

Key Protocol Points — Headache AO

  • Thorough headache history using OPQRST
  • Screen for red flag features (see below)
  • Measure BGL — exclude hypoglycaemia as a cause
  • Perform ROSIER assessment if any neurological symptoms or signs
  • Pain management: Paracetamol 1g PO (if not contraindicated)
  • Nausea management: Ondansetron ODT if indicated
  • Escalate to EOC Clinician for all red flag presentations
  • Transport to appropriate facility — consider stroke pathway per CPP-016-AO

Red Flag Headache Features

Red Flags — Any of these warrant escalation:
  • Sudden onset, severe — "thunderclap" headache
  • "Worst headache of my life"
  • New neurological deficit (facial droop, limb weakness, speech disturbance, visual loss)
  • Altered consciousness or confusion
  • Fever with neck stiffness (meningism)
  • Recent significant head trauma
  • New-type headache in patient over 50 years
  • Progressive worsening over days/weeks
  • Headache worse with coughing, straining, or bending
  • Seizure associated with headache

ROSIER Scale (Recognition of Stroke in the Emergency Room)

FeatureScore
Loss of consciousness or syncope-1
Seizure activity-1
New acute onset: Asymmetric facial weakness+1
New acute onset: Asymmetric arm weakness+1

Score of 1 or more = stroke possible. Score of 0 or less = stroke less likely, but does not exclude serious pathology.

Critical Teaching Point: A negative ROSIER score does NOT exclude a life-threatening cause of headache. ROSIER screens for stroke — it does not screen for subarachnoid haemorrhage. SAH frequently presents without focal neurological deficit. The red flag features in the history are what drive your clinical concern, not the ROSIER result alone.

Red Flags Identified in This Patient

  • Sudden onset, severe headache — thunderclap pattern
  • "Worst headache of my life"
  • New-type headache in a patient over 50
  • Developing neck stiffness (meningism)
  • Nausea and photophobia
  • Elevated blood pressure

Expected Working Diagnosis

  • Primary concern: Possible subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) — requires urgent CT imaging
  • Differentials to consider: Hypertensive emergency with headache, meningitis (though afebrile), migraine (no prior history, thunderclap onset atypical)

Key Teaching Points

  • ROSIER is one tool, not the whole answer. It tells you about stroke. The headache protocol asks you to assess broader red flags.
  • A thunderclap headache is subarachnoid haemorrhage until proven otherwise by CT scan — that is not something you can do in the field.
  • Do not be falsely reassured by a negative ROSIER. Your clinical concern based on the history is valid and must drive your actions.
  • This patient needs to get to hospital for definitive investigation. Your job is to recognise, stabilise, and transport safely.

Real-Job Translation

On a real job, you may feel uncertain because the ROSIER is negative and the patient "looks okay." Trust the red flags. If the history says thunderclap, worst ever, new onset, with neck stiffness — that patient is going to hospital. Call it early.

Skill Practice — Paracetamol PO Administration

Walk through the following steps aloud and demonstrate with equipment:

  1. Confirm indication: Headache pain, severity 9/10. Paracetamol is appropriate first-line analgesia within AO scope.
  2. Confirm dose: 1g (2 x 500mg tablets).
  3. Five rights check:
    • Right patient — confirm name (Graham Thompson)
    • Right drug — read label: "Paracetamol 500mg"
    • Right dose — 1g (2 tablets)
    • Right route — oral (PO)
    • Right time — confirm when patient last took Paracetamol
  4. Screen for contraindications:
    • Known hypersensitivity to Paracetamol? — No
    • Severe hepatic impairment? — No
    • Has patient taken Paracetamol in the last 4 hours? — Ask patient and wife. Graham confirms he has not taken any today.
    • Maximum 4g in 24 hours — dose is safe to administer.
  5. Confirm with patient: "Graham, I'd like to give you some Paracetamol for the pain. Two tablets with a sip of water. Are you able to swallow safely?"
  6. Administer: Hand tablets to patient with a small cup of water. Observe the patient swallowing.
  7. Document: Time administered, drug, dose, route, indication, and any observations. Record on ePCR or case sheet.
  8. Plan reassessment: Reassess pain in 15–20 minutes.
Medication Safety Reminder: Always ask about recent Paracetamol intake. Patients with chronic pain conditions, such as Graham's osteoarthritis, may have already self-medicated before calling an ambulance. Exceeding 4g in 24 hours risks hepatotoxicity.

Ondansetron ODT for Nausea

If the patient reports significant nausea (Graham does), consider Ondansetron ODT 4mg:

  1. Confirm indication: nausea associated with headache.
  2. Check contraindications: known hypersensitivity, congenital long QT syndrome.
  3. Administer: Place orally disintegrating tablet on the tongue. Allow to dissolve. No water required.
  4. Document: Time, drug, dose, route.

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

  • Reduce environmental stimuli — move into shaded area or inside the caravan if safe to do so
  • Dim lighting / close blinds if moving inside
  • Reduce noise — ask bystanders to give space
  • Position of comfort — patient may prefer sitting upright or semi-reclined with head elevated
  • Cool cloth to forehead if tolerated
  • Calm, quiet communication — avoid loud or rapid speech

They recommend close monitoring of GCS and neurological status during transport. No additional medications authorised at this stage. They will pre-notify the hospital if requested.

Key Teaching Points

  • Paracetamol will not significantly relieve the pain of a subarachnoid haemorrhage — but it is within your scope and demonstrates you have addressed analgesia.
  • The real treatment for this patient is getting to a CT scanner. Everything you do on scene is stabilisation and preparation for transport.
  • Non-pharmacological interventions matter. Reducing light, noise, and stimulation is genuine clinical care for this patient.
  • Document your EOC Clinician consultation — time called, advice given, clinician name if provided.

Real-Job Translation

On a real job, you may feel pressure to "do more." For this patient, recognising the red flags, initiating what you can, and making the decision to transport promptly is doing the most important thing. Scene time matters — do not delay transport for marginal interventions.

Decision-Making

Reassessment and Escalation Decision

Facilitator Prompts

  • "What has changed between your first and second set of observations?"
  • "What does a GCS drop from 15 to 14 mean in this clinical context?"
  • "Has your level of clinical concern changed? How?"
  • "Do you need to re-contact the EOC Clinician?"

Expected AO Reasoning

  • GCS 15 to 14 represents a clinically significant change — the patient is deteriorating
  • Confusion developing in a thunderclap headache patient increases suspicion for expanding SAH or re-bleed
  • Rising BP may reflect increasing intracranial pressure (Cushing response)
  • This is now time-critical — minimise remaining scene time
  • Re-contact EOC Clinician to update on deterioration
  • Consider requesting paramedic backup to intercept if available
  • Do NOT delay transport waiting for backup — load and go

Key Teaching Points

  • Trending observations is how you detect deterioration. A single set of observations is a snapshot — two sets give you a direction of travel.
  • A GCS drop of 1 point may not sound dramatic, but any decline in consciousness in this context is significant and must be communicated.
  • The combination of worsening GCS + rising BP + headache is a concerning triad that suggests rising intracranial pressure.
  • Escalation is not a sign of failure — it is a core clinical skill. Update the EOC Clinician promptly.

Real-Job Translation

On a real job, you might second-guess yourself: "Is he just tired? Is the Paracetamol making him drowsy?" Trust the numbers. If GCS has dropped, document it, report it, and act on it. You would rather escalate and be wrong than not escalate and miss a deterioration.

Extrication and Transport

The decision is made to load and transport to Port Lincoln Hospital. Sandra (wife) will follow in their car. She is given the hospital address and told to drive carefully.

Break Point
Practical Skill / Discussion

Extrication and Transport Planning

Facilitator Prompts

  • "How are you going to move Graham from the deck chair to the stretcher?"
  • "What position should the patient be transported in?"
  • "What are your monitoring priorities during transport?"
  • "Who is in the back and who is driving?"
  • "What is your transport code?"

Expected AO Reasoning

  • Extrication method: Stretcher brought to the patient (Site 47 is accessible). Assist Graham to stand and pivot onto the stretcher. If unsteady due to confusion, use a standing pivot with both AOs supporting — minimise exertion for the patient.
  • Position: Supine with head elevated approximately 30 degrees — reduces intracranial pressure while maintaining comfort.
  • In the back: Clinician (AO1) — continuous patient monitoring, GCS checks every 5 minutes, observe for vomiting (aspiration risk with declining consciousness).
  • Driver (AO2): Smooth, steady driving. Lights and sirens (Priority 1) given clinical deterioration.
  • Monitoring during transport: Continuous Corpuls3 (HR, SpO2, ECG), 5-minute GCS checks, watch for signs of further deterioration — vomiting, seizure, decreasing consciousness.
  • Prepare for worst case: Suction assembled and within reach. Recovery position plan if vomiting with decreased consciousness. BVM accessible.
Airway Consideration: A patient with declining GCS and nausea is at risk of vomiting and aspiration. Have suction assembled, tested, and within arm's reach for the entire transport. Know your plan if GCS drops further — lateral position, suction, BVM ready.

Key Teaching Points

  • Head-up positioning (30 degrees) is a simple, evidence-based intervention for patients with suspected raised intracranial pressure.
  • Smooth transport matters — avoid sudden braking, hard turns, and potholes where possible. Communicate with your driver.
  • If the patient deteriorates to GCS less than or equal to 12, or loses airway patency, this becomes a critical patient. Update EOC immediately.
  • Have your contingency plan before you leave the scene, not during a crisis at 110km/h on the Lincoln Highway.

Real-Job Translation

The drive from Coffin Bay to Port Lincoln Hospital is approximately 35 minutes. That is a long time alone in the back with a deteriorating patient. Prepare for the worst. Suction ready. BVM ready. Know your escalation triggers. Communicate with your driver through the partition.

Handover

You arrive at Port Lincoln Hospital Emergency Department at approximately 15:15. The ED has been pre-notified via the EOC Clinician. A nurse meets you at the ambulance bay.

Break Point
Practical Skill

ISBAR Handover

Facilitator Prompts

  • "AO1 — deliver your ISBAR handover aloud as if the facilitator is the receiving nurse."
  • "Make sure you communicate the deterioration and your clinical concern."
  • "What specific request do you make of the receiving team?"

Model ISBAR Handover

I — Identify

"Good afternoon, I'm [name], Ambulance Officer from Coffin Bay. This is Graham Thompson, 62 years old. His wife Sandra is following by car."

S — Situation

"Graham developed a sudden onset severe headache at approximately 13:40 today while sitting outside at the Coffin Bay Caravan Park. He describes it as the worst headache of his life — thunderclap onset with no preceding trauma or exertion. He has deteriorated during our care with a GCS drop from 15 to 14."

B — Background

"He has a history of hypertension managed with Perindopril 5mg daily and mild osteoarthritis. He is allergic to Penicillin — causes a rash. He has no history of previous severe headaches or migraine. On assessment, he had nausea, photophobia, and developing neck stiffness. ROSIER score was 0 — no focal neurological deficit."

A — Assessment

"Current observations: heart rate 92, BP 182/106, respiratory rate 20, SpO2 97% on room air, GCS 14 — he is confused. BGL was 6.8. Pupils equal and reactive. He has received Paracetamol 1 gram PO at 14:38 and Ondansetron 4mg ODT at 14:40. Pain reduced from 9 to 8 out of 10. GCS dropped from 15 to 14 during our care. BP has trended upward."

R — Recommendation

"We are concerned about possible subarachnoid haemorrhage given the thunderclap onset, worst-ever headache, neck stiffness, and now declining consciousness. We believe he needs urgent CT imaging. The EOC Clinician has been consulted and supports this transport. Sandra, his wife, should arrive within 10 minutes."

Expected AO Reasoning

  • ISBAR is structured and concise — deliver it in under 90 seconds
  • Lead with the critical information: thunderclap headache, deteriorating GCS
  • State your clinical concern clearly: "We are concerned about possible subarachnoid haemorrhage"
  • Include trending data: "GCS dropped from 15 to 14", "BP has trended upward"
  • Mention the EOC Clinician consultation — this adds weight to your assessment
  • Include family information — Sandra is en route

Key Teaching Points

  • A good handover tells the receiving team what you found, what you did, and what you are worried about.
  • Stating your working diagnosis ("possible SAH") is appropriate and expected. You are not diagnosing — you are communicating your clinical concern.
  • Include the time of symptom onset. For a potential SAH, this is critical for the receiving team's decision-making.
  • Trending observations in your handover (GCS 15 to 14, BP rising) communicates a trajectory, not just a snapshot.

Real-Job Translation

In a busy rural ED, you may be handing over to a nurse who then relays to the doctor. Make your handover clear enough that it survives being repeated. If in doubt, ask: "Can I speak with the doctor directly? I'm concerned about a declining patient."

Debrief (10–15 minutes)

Reflective Questions

1. At what point did you first suspect this was not a simple headache? What information triggered that concern?
2. The ROSIER score was negative. How did you manage the tension between a "negative" screening tool and your clinical suspicion?
3. What would you have done differently if Graham had been confused from the outset — GCS 13 on your arrival?
4. Was there anything that could have reduced your on-scene time? What was essential and what could have been done during transport?
5. How did your crew communication work? Were roles clear? Was there a moment where better communication would have changed the flow?
6. If Graham had vomited and his GCS dropped to 10 during transport, what would your immediate actions have been?
7. How confident do you feel delivering an ISBAR handover that includes a working diagnosis you cannot confirm with imaging? What supports that confidence?

Take-Home Messages

1A thunderclap headache — sudden onset, maximal severity within seconds — is subarachnoid haemorrhage until CT proves otherwise. This is a time-critical presentation.
2A negative ROSIER score does not equal a safe patient. ROSIER screens for stroke. SAH, meningitis, and other life-threatening causes of headache can have a ROSIER score of zero.
3Trending observations detects deterioration that a single snapshot misses. A GCS drop of even one point is clinically significant in this context and must be communicated.
4Red flag headache features drive your escalation and transport decisions. Learn them, screen for them on every headache job, and act on them when present.
5Your job is to recognise, stabilise within scope, and transport to definitive care. The CT scanner at the hospital is the treatment — getting the patient there promptly is the most important thing you do.
6Non-pharmacological care is genuine clinical care. Reducing light, noise, and stimulation for a photophobic, nauseated patient is not soft — it is appropriate management.
7Always have a deterioration plan before you leave the scene. Suction ready, BVM accessible, lateral position plan rehearsed. The back of an ambulance on the Lincoln Highway is not the place to think about this for the first time.

Variation 3 — Post-Traumatic Headache

Change the scenario: Graham tripped over a guy rope 2 hours ago and hit his head on the caravan step. He initially felt fine but has developed a progressively worsening headache, now with nausea and one episode of vomiting. He is on Warfarin for atrial fibrillation.

Focus: Post-traumatic headache with anticoagulant use, risk of intracranial haemorrhage, C-spine considerations, GCS monitoring, urgency of transport for CT and INR check.

Variation 4 — Rapid Deterioration During Transport

Use the original scenario but during transport Graham's GCS drops to 9 (E2 V3 M4). He vomits and is unable to protect his own airway. SpO2 drops to 91%.

Focus: Airway management in a deteriorating patient, lateral positioning, suction, BVM ventilation, immediate EOC Clinician update, crew communication through the partition, requesting paramedic intercept or MedSTAR advice, continuing transport vs. stopping.
