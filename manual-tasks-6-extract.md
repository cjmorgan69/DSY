## 8. Scenario: Manual Tasks Risk Assessment
Mrs Jones is 96 and lives in an independent living unit. She has poor eyesight and had several near misses with falls. She weighs approximately 75 kilograms and normally walks with a frame when she is outside.

You and your partner have responded to a call out and find Mrs Jones on the floor in her bathroom. She has slipped on a wet patch by the sink. Your assessment reveals she is physically unharmed, and you are now planning how to get her from the floor onto a chair in the kitchen.

The safest way to undertake a manual task is as shown in the 'hierarchy of control' diagram for Manual Tasks. (Button: Hierarchy of Control.)

Please note, the patient has been clinically assessed to determine there are no injuries or medical conditions to contraindicate this transfer.

## 9. Manual Tasks Risk Assessment and Control (Hierarchy of Control)
A pyramid with six levels, most effective at the top, least effective at the bottom. Click on each level for more information:

1. **Eliminate the Tasks** (most effective). The most effective way would be eliminating the manual task completely. If assessed as safe to do so, ask the patient to get up from the floor with the Ambulance Officer prompting them on how to do this using furniture. The Ambulance Officer would be there to steady the patient if they lost their balance.
2. **Alter the Workplace or Environmental Conditions.** This is usually a prevention strategy, for example, the patient slipped over as they were trying to stand up from their armchair wearing socks with no shoes. Always putting slippers on or wearing 'safety socks' with grips on the soles could have prevented her fall in the first place.
3. **Alter the Systems of Work.** An example could be SAAS receiving frequent call outs from a supportive living complex for a patient who regularly falls. If the complex purchases their own equipment to assist with getting fallen residents off the floor, this would be 'altering the systems of work'.
4. **Change the Object Used In the Task.** This could include using two kitchen chairs with the transfer rather than one chair. For example, one in front for support to lift off the floor and one to slip under her bottom as she stands.
5. **Use Mechanical Aids.** In some situations, you may be required to use a Camel Lifting Device, or Raizer Patient Lifter.
6. **Provide Information and Training In Manual Handling Techniques** (least effective). Part of this is this eLearning package you are undertaking, and the practicals you will complete as part of your face to face training.

## 11. Dynamic Risk Assessment
When we are on shift we are constantly monitoring our surroundings and controlling risks at a scene. We want to minimise the likelihood of injury to SAAS workers, patients, and bystanders.

Dynamic risk assessment is the intuitive process used to control the risk at a scene and reduce the likelihood of injury to SAAS staff, patients and bystanders. It consists of identifying hazards, assessing risks and mitigating those risks with the best controls available during changing circumstances, also known as a 'risk assessment on the run'.

The R.E.A.D mnemonic is adapted from Ambulance Victoria to guide you in the process of Dynamic Risk Assessment at a scene. **READ the situation:** click on the buttons to view each word in the mnemonic:
- **R** = Risks
- **E** = Equipment
- **A** = Action Plan
- **D** = Discuss
