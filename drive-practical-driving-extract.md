## 3. Observation Alarm
- Video (1:38): https://player.vimeo.com/video/850365492?h=d392f8696d

An observation alarm is an internal alarm or subconscious trigger that should remind you that you have less than 5 seconds of forward vision available.

It is not the gap between you and the vehicle in front, but the extent of your vision as you travel along the road.

## 4. Observation Alarm (2)
Over time this alarm will become a subconscious process. It is a warning that gives your brain more time to plan a response.

5 seconds = 83 metres

Consider changing lanes. By scanning ahead at least five seconds you then have the opportunity to:
- Move into a clearer lane
- Get around a heavy vehicle
- Clear obstructions early

## 5. Observation Link
An observation link is where you make a connection between something you can see and something that may happen because of that vision. Watch the animation on this slide to see three examples:
- A ball in the road... will a child run out?
- A bus stopped at a bus stop... will a pedestrian walk out into the road?
- Bins put out on the side of the road... will the lane be obstructed by a garbage truck?

## 6. Blind Spots
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/850369037?h=14b192c1b3 (layer "Video: Ambulance Blind Spots", 1:25)

Blind spots obscure your vision. They are created from the way that the vehicle has been designed, or through the placement of in vehicle equipment. Our ambulances have a number of these.

To mitigate these, you must:
- Know where the blind spots are.
- Adjust your seat and mirrors correctly.
- Move your eyes and your head around any in vehicle obstructions on the approach to any other road user or obstruction.
- Use mirror checks and shoulder checks for any manoeuvre.

## 7. Blockouts

Blockouts are objects outside of your vehicle that obstruct your view. These can be mitigated through:
- Careful observation: look through, look around, look under, look twice.
- Adjust your speed.
- Hang back from large vehicles.
- Move vehicle, right or left, forward or backward.
- Position 'wide' for a corner.

## 8. Forecasting and Planning
Forecasting is the ability to view the road and traffic ahead by looking for the evidence from the driver's actions and position. This should lead to the Ambulance driver making an accurate assessment of what is going to happen.

Once forecasting has taken place then the next stage is to have a plan as to what the ambulance driver's actions are going to be.

In routine driving this will be around driving to a position of advantage, driving to increase vision and be in the best possible position to see all of the hazards / threats that will be negotiated at the intersection.

During emergency driving, planning may involve using options provided under Australian road rule 306 in order to progress the journey.

## 10. Scanning and Looking
- Video "Scanning Sequence" (1:04): https://player.vimeo.com/video/856634784?h=1cf9f324c1
- Second video (Looking): https://player.vimeo.com/video/861091695?h=2390a421ff

As a driver you must be able to differentiate between 'scanning' and 'looking'. Scanning gives you a broad overview of what is going on, when you look is when you see the details.

**Scanning.** Scanning is the systematic approach to gathering important information in order to have good overall vision and awareness around the vehicle to see hazards. Scanning is a big picture that lacks details but will highlight areas / threats that need to be looked at.

**Looking.** Where scanning gives you an 'overview' picture, it does not contain detail. When we look we are gathering evidence and details as to whether it is safe to continue, or whether to make adjustments to our driving to ensure safe passage.

**Scanning sequence.** The scanning sequence will help to ensure you don't miss anything. During your practical drive you will be asked to apply these principles. To help you master this, a 10 point checklist has been developed:
1. Look as far ahead as possible from the front of the vehicle scanning the road surface.
2. Continue forward to as far as you can see down the road.
3 and 4. Look at both sides of the road from far to near.
5 and 6. Look up every side street and entrance on both sides of the road where a vehicle may reasonably be expected.
7, 8 and 9. Look to the rear of the vehicle from the left to the centre. Look at the driver's side mirror and over the shoulder.
10. Finally, look at the instruments and dash display, note the speedometer.

## 11. Crash Avoidance Space
- Video "CAS Explained": https://player.vimeo.com/video/861090318?h=51268ce32b

Your crash avoidance space (CAS) is the distance between you and the vehicle in front. The length of the crash avoidance space is speed dependent.

CAS = Reaction Time + Braking Time

Minimum 2 seconds. When driving at 60 km/h in good conditions, when you are alert and in a well maintained vehicle, your CAS should be at least 2 seconds. When travelling above 60 km/h in the above conditions, your CAS must be extended out to 3 seconds.

## 12. Crash Avoidance Space (2)
(Layer video "Recognise the Threat - CAS Explained": no separate link found; probably the same video as slide 11.)

When driving in adverse conditions you should increase the distance of your CAS. Adverse conditions are considered cumulative, so for each element present add an additional 1 second to your CAS. For example a dark road add 1 second, whereas on a dark wet road add 2 seconds.

Minimum 4 seconds.

## 13. Hazard Versus Threat
- Video "Example of Hazard Versus Threat": https://player.vimeo.com/video/857754975?h=4adcd021cc

Hazards and threats are two completely different things.

A hazard is the precursor to anything in your driving environment that could later become a threat to your safety. Hazard recognition is the early part of being able to recognise an actual 'threat' to your safety. A hazard becomes a threat when it is about to enter your crash avoidance space. The threat is then mitigated, as much as possible, with evidence gathering and applying options to minimise a threat.

**Threat.** An identified hazard becomes a threat to you when it is about to enter your CAS. A threat is anything that can negatively impact on your safety or progress.
If you do NOT recognise threats to your safety whilst you are driving, you are driving in an inattentive manner, and you cannot justify the risks you are taking.
When faced with a threat you must collect evidence and if necessary, take action to minimise the threat to your driving. Do not leave your safety in the hands of other road users.
If you find that you must brake unexpectedly or change direction quickly then you have failed to recognise a threat to your safety. If you are driving and suddenly catch something out of the corner of your eye and you do not have enough time to do anything about it then you are failing to observe the information that the road and traffic conditions are giving you.
As the driver of an emergency vehicle, you must not be surprised by anything in your driving environment, your driving style should be calm, deliberate and always under complete control.

**Hazard.** Hazards can be made up of anything in your driving environment; from another road user to poor road conditions to bad weather. Whole areas that are blocked from vision (blind spots or block outs) present a hazard as you cannot plan until you can see. Intersections are always a hazard.
Hazards are always beyond the stopping distance of the vehicle that you are driving. You must select pieces of information that let your brain know that you are nearing a place a crash could happen or a hazard that has yet to be assessed. If that is still some distance away, you might have to leave your closer look until you get nearer to it.
Once you have observed a hazard it is important to calculate when it becomes a threat to your safety and how you are going to reduce the chance of any harm happening to you as you prepare to negotiate the hazard.

## 14. Evidence Gathering
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/857752258?h=9e1cb013d7

When a threat needs to be negotiated you must collect suitable evidence in order to develop the best plans to manage it. The phrase 'Eyes, Hands, Wheels' involves looking at the threat to get the evidence needed.
- **Eyes:** Think about any barriers to observation, for example sun glare, fog, etc. Can you see? Can the other road user see?
- **Hands:** Think about the complete body language of the driver / pedestrians. Does the body language forecast what actions that person might take?
- **Wheels:** Think about any movement you can see from the position of their vehicle. What is this movement telling you?

If you can get a clear indication from each of these areas then it is probably safe to continue your path without making any adjustment. If in any doubt then you must take action to minimise the threat.

## 15. Minimising a Threat
- Video "Options to minimise a Threat": https://player.vimeo.com/video/850795521?h=b1130e5010

The following are some actions you can take to minimise a threat:
- **Steer to a safer position.** Move away from the threat and buy more time and space. Make sure that you do not drive into another area of risk.
- **Cover the brake.** Hover your foot over the brake pedal. You buy more time reducing your physical reaction time.
- **Use a warning device.** Headlights and horn must be used legally and not as a rebuke.
- **Accelerate.** On rare occasions accelerating can put you further away from a hazard. For these risks, slowing down may have placed you on a collision course. Always consider what happened to put you in a position where you needed to accelerate to get away from a threat.
- **Slow or stop if necessary.**
- **Set up the brake.** Remove the free play from the braking system and illuminate the brake lights. You have prepared the vehicle and your passengers for firm and controlled braking and alerted any drivers behind you.
- **Ease up the accelerator.** Doing this allows more time for the threat to dissipate or their actions to become clear, you reduce the surprise factor.

## 16. Unjustifiable Risk
There are two ways an unjustifiable risk can occur:
- When the driver fails to recognise threats to their safety whilst driving. This driving can be seen as inattentive and potentially dangerous, and the driver cannot justify the risks they are taking.
- If a driver sees a threat but fails to mitigate it (getting evidence / using an option to minimise the threat) this is also seen as an unjustifiable risk.

## 17. Stable Platform
The nine DRiVE principles shown: Scanning; Cornering; Road positioning; Driving to a position of advantage; Braking; Forecasting and planning; Setup; Manoeuvring; Crash avoidance space.

The ability for an ambulance driver to convey their partner and a patient in a safe and calm manner despite the road and traffic conditions or urgency of the transport is paramount.

A stable platform is a result of the proper application of all DRiVE principles. These must be incorporated into every drive in order to produce the required stable platform essential for our work.

## 18. Position of Advantage
The vehicle should always be in a position on the road that allows for best vision and progression. This is a dynamic process and needs to be constantly considered.

Position of advantage encompasses:
- Anticipated flow of traffic around intersections and junctions
- Choosing lanes with no block outs
- Choosing lanes with the most forward progression of traffic

## 19. Three Pressure Braking
- Video "Three Pressure Braking" (1:09): https://player.vimeo.com/video/850393447?h=776b3cb4de

When applied correctly, your vehicle will come to a complete stop without it being abrupt or causing the front suspension to recoil.

To achieve three pressure braking you must: set up the brake, squeeze the brake then ease the brake. This technique is essential for a smooth ride and stable platform and should be your standard way of applying the brakes for all drives.

Click on each component to learn more:
1. **Setting up the brake**
   1. Ease accelerator gently.
   2. Remove initial free-play from pedal.
   3. Minimal speed change: weight transfers forward, increase front tyre grip, brake lights illuminate.
   4. Cover contact plate with ball of foot.
2. **Squeeze the brake**
   1. Squeeze brake pedal progressively for the required braking.
   2. Speed reduced as tyre grip is progressively used.
   3. Routine braking should never activate an ABS system.
3. **Ease the brake**
   1. When almost stopped ease the brake pedal pressure slightly just before you come to a stop.
   2. Achieving a stop with no abrupt final jolt.
   3. Brake should still be applied after you ease the pressure and not come completely off.

## 21. Threshold and ABS Braking
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/856649345?h=e938099f1f
- Layer "Video: ABS Braking" (video shows a full ABS brake from 100 kph; link not separately found)

Threshold braking is the understanding of, and the application of maximum braking effort without the application of the vehicle's ABS system. Three pressure braking should still be applied when using threshold braking.

ABS (anti-lock braking system) braking (emergency braking), if required, is full brake with ABS activation.

**Braking distance.** When a braking point is set and there isn't any "surprise element" (for example a child running out onto the road) at 80 km/hr the distance to stop under both ABS and threshold braking is approximately 27 metres. If reaction time was added to this situation the distance to stop would increase.

When ABS braking is activated the braking effect is quite violent and rough leading to a very uncomfortable stop for patients and passengers. When a similar force of braking is carried out but maintaining threshold braking (maximum brake pressure just prior to ABS application) the stop is within the same distance as an ABS stop but it is in a smoother manner.

**ABS braking.** Being in a heavier vehicle the braking distances we relate to in good conditions are:
- At 60 kph = 30 metres
- At 80 kph = 55 metres
- At 110 kph = 80 metres

The video shows a full ABS brake from 100 kph. The reason for the ABS function is to stop the wheels from locking and thereby giving the driver the ability to steer around an obstacle. The same effect can be done with threshold braking as you can apply maximum braking but not lock the wheels.

(The 27 m figure at 80 km/h and the 55 m figure at 80 kph appear on the same slide and disagree. The slide may mean the first is for a light vehicle or a different condition. Check before using.)

## 22. Accelerator Sense
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/850429533?h=d9101259e2

Involves the ability to vary the speed of the vehicle to match the changing traffic conditions without using the brake.

Acceleration should be smooth and progressive, allowing for easing of the accelerator just prior to the automatic gear changes.

It involves the ability to negotiate the driving environment while using minimal braking and maintaining the correct crash avoidance space.

## 23. Corralling (spelled "Coralling" in the slide title)
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/852556892?h=1f3004746b

Rear end crashes are the most common type of crashes, and a large percentage are due to driver distraction.

Corralling vehicles is using a combination of accelerator sense and three pressure braking to bring the vehicles behind in a bit closer so that their speed matches your speed before coming to a final stop.

Stopping when there are no vehicles behind you must involve leaving enough room so the ambulance can roll forwards to lessen the chance of being rear-ended.

Being aware of the vehicles behind you can help with this, but corralling also involves good forwards scanning and interpretation of traffic patterns.

## 24. Reversing
- Video: https://player.vimeo.com/video/851462333?h=f9e1a9dfb1

Reversing is the highest cause of crash rates in SAAS vehicles. Ideally you as a driver should position the vehicle so that the need to attempt a reverse manoeuvre is reduced as much as possible.

The ambulance vehicles have a number of features to increase the safety of reversing. These include: reversing beeper; white rear scan light; reversing camera.

Where possible reversing is a two person manoeuvre, with the second person used to signal progress through the reversing manoeuvre.

The application of the steering must always be smooth and precise.
- Push/pull and rotational steering are both accepted or a combination of them both.
- Palm steering is not accepted due to the risk of hands slipping on the wheel and reduced vehicle control.
- Steering must be associated with appropriate vision in the direction the vehicle is travelling.
- Thumbs should always be around the wheel and if there are thumb channels provided, they should be used.

## 26. Cornering
- Videos: https://player.vimeo.com/video/863392283?h=a9a3479cdc ; https://player.vimeo.com/video/863395058?h=61eebd4372 ; https://player.vimeo.com/video/862991769?h=2e8ca849d0
  (layers "Video: Cornering Vision Point" and "Video: Reading a Bend Using Vision Point"; third likely the corner positioning clip)

Two key concepts for safe and effective cornering are:
- The driver must always be able to stop within the distance that you can see to be clear, and
- The driver must match their vehicle speed to the speed at which the vision-point changes, and this must be done with a smooth balance of steering, accelerator, and brake use.

Six clickable topics: Corner Positioning; Vision Point; Reading a Bend Using Vision Point; Tyre Grip; Reserves of Grip; Cornering Radius.

**Corner positioning.** When cornering on a right bend, move to the left of the lane to increase the vision around the bend. When cornering around a left bend, move to the right of the lane to increase vision around the bend.

**Vision point.** The vision point is where the left side of the road meets the right side of the road. It is the last bit of bitumen that can be seen from the driver's position as you head towards a corner. The vision point will do one of three things on your approach:
- If the vision point is moving away from you the corner is getting less and you can begin to straighten the steering which also allows for gentle application of the accelerator as you come out of the corner.
- If the vision point is getting closer to you it is telling you that the corner is tight. You must always be able to stop in the distance you can see to be clear, so in this instance you would be slowing on the approach to this corner.
- If the vision point is moving ahead of you at the same rate that you are negotiating the corner, then the corner is at a steady radius and as long as you can safely stop in the distance you have between you and the vision point then you can maintain drive to the wheels at the speed you are now travelling at.

**Reading a bend using vision point** (5 steps, with video):
1. *Approaching the bend.* At first, the vision point (black arrow) appears to remain at the same point in the road. Reduce your speed to be able to stop safely within the remaining distance. As you approach the bend take information about the sharpness of the bend and carefully assess the appropriate speed for cornering.
2. *Going through the bend.* Just before you enter the bend, the vision point begins to move round at a constant speed. Adjust your speed to the speed of this movement. You now have the correct speed for the bend.
3. *Coming out of the bend.* As the bend starts to straighten out your view begins to extend, and the vision point starts to move away more quickly. You then can accelerate towards the vision point in proportion to the straightening out of your steering. As the bend comes to an end, continue to accelerate to catch the vision point until other considerations such as speed limits or new hazards restrict your acceleration.
4. *Cornering radius.* Increasing the radius of any corner lessens the forces affecting the vehicle, and reducing the speed at which a corner is taken also lessens the forces affecting the vehicle and its occupants. It is important to know, when you 'coast' around a corner, particularly in a vehicle with a high centre of gravity (i.e. an ambulance), the vehicle will tend to roll on its suspension and become uncomfortable for your partner and patient. This also occurs if you enter a corner at too high a speed and have to remain on the brake deep into the corner or bend. Use your accelerator to maintain drive to the wheels as you negotiate the bend/corner. Do not accelerate faster throughout the corner. Changing speed throughout a corner or bend changes the steering path so steering adjustments will have to be made which may make the vehicle unstable and be uncomfortable to those in the back. Slow down and you may oversteer, speed up and understeer.

**Tyre grip.** To use tyre grip efficiently you have to make intelligent use of the small 'patch' of tyre that contacts the road. This contact patch, which is only about 11 x 16 cm on the Mercedes, has a limited amount of grip on the road. The way you brake, steer and accelerate will make it either harder or easier for this small patch of tyre to grip the road.

**Reserves of grip** (3 steps):
1. To maintain safe smooth negotiation of a corner or bend the driver must balance inputs of speed and turning. Using an 'input' score just like a 'pain score' on a scale from 1 to 10 can help to explain this.
   - The driver has 10 units of steering input available: 0 = straight and 10 = hard lock.
   - The driver has 10 units of braking input available: 0 = no braking and 10 = locked brakes.
   - The driver has 10 units of acceleration input available: 0 = no acceleration and 10 = foot to the floor.
2. The driver must never exceed 10 units of combined steering and braking or acceleration in any corner. If more than a combined total of 10 units are applied to the vehicle whilst negotiating a corner/bend, then the chances of the driver/vehicle losing control, crashing or creating a very uncomfortable ride are dramatically increased. The driver of a SAAS vehicle should aim to use a maximum of 8 units of combined inputs. This allows for a reserve if an error is made and also allows for a stable platform to be maintained.
3. If at the beginning of the corner it is very tight, the driver will have to apply a lot of steering input (7 units of steering), this leaves only marginal scope to use the brake. Likewise, if a driver is braking hard on the approach to a corner using 8 units of their braking potential, the ability to turn the vehicle into the corner is very limited. Safe and smooth cornering is all about a balance of inputs. For safest practice, braking should be done before the corner so that the vehicle enters the corner/bend at a safe speed. It is also important not to brake heavy and then release the brake rapidly just as the driver enters the corner as this will cause a shift in the vehicle's mechanical forces and the vehicle will be harder to "turn in" to the corner.
